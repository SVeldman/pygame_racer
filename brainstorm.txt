how hard would it be to make a verticle scrolling racer that has the player competing against other racers?

Making a vertical scrolling racer with AI competitors is moderately challenging (Intermediate level). It is a fantastic project because it feels like a "real" arcade game, but the math is much simpler than a top-down game where cars can rotate in 360 degrees.In a vertical scroller, the player's car usually stays locked in a forward-facing position, and you simulate forward speed by moving the road and other cars downward.Here is a breakdown of why it is achievable and what makes it tricky:Why it is easier than it looksNo Complex Physics: You do not need to program drifting, turning radiuses, or complex angles. Cars move strictly on a 2D grid: up, down, left, and right.Built-in Hitboxes: Pygame Zero handles collision detection effortlessly. If a competitor hits the player, a simple if player.colliderect(enemy): statement handles the crash.The Challenging PartsSimulating a "Race": Since the background loops infinitely, you aren't tracking a physical finish line on a map. Instead, you track a "distance traveled" variable. The race finishes when that variable hits a target number (e.g., 5,000 meters).Coding the AI Competitors: Making other cars look like they are racing requires a bit of logic. They need to spawn ahead of you, change lanes randomly, and react to the player's speed.Relative Speed Math: This is the core trick. If the player accelerates, the other cars need to move downward faster. If the player slows down, the other cars should shoot forward off the top of the screen.How the AI Logic Works (The Blueprint)To make the AI feel alive, you give each opponent car a base_speed. Then, you calculate its actual screen movement using relative speed:python# In your update loop for each opponent car:
enemy.y += (player_speed - enemy.base_speed)
Use code with caution.If you drive faster than the AI (player_speed = 10, enemy.base_speed = 7), the result is positive (+3), so the enemy slowly drifts downward toward the bottom of the screen. You are passing them!If you drive slower than the AI (player_speed = 4, enemy.base_speed = 7), the result is negative (-3), so the enemy moves upward and pulls away from you.Would you like to see a bare-bones working code template for this? how hard would it be to turn this into a split-screen multiplayer race for the advanced students?

Turning this into a split-screen multiplayer game is a noticeable step up in difficulty, but it is a perfect, highly engaging extension for your advanced students who finish early.It transitions the project from an Intermediate level to an Advanced level.The challenge isn't the multiplayer logic itself (which just uses different keyboard keys like W, A, S, D vs. Arrow Keys). The real challenge is how Pygame Zero handles rendering. Pygame Zero does not have a native "camera" or "viewports" system to easily split the screen in half.Here is how it can be achieved, the technical hurdles, and a clever workaround.📐 The Technical HurdlesNo Canvas Splitting: Normally, in complex engines, you create two viewports and draw player 1's world on the left and player 2's world on the right. In Pygame Zero, everything draws directly to one flat window.Independent Progress: Player 1 might be speeding ahead while Player 2 crashes and slows down. This means you must track two separate scrolling backgrounds and two separate sets of enemy relative speeds.💡 Two Ways to Approach ItDepending on how advanced your students are, you can choose between a "True Split-Screen" workaround or a "Single-Screen Shared Track" alternative.Option A: The "True" Split-Screen Workaround (Harder)To achieve a literal split-screen, students must use advanced Python coordinate math to restrict where images are drawn.The Math: They will need to split the screen window down the middle (e.g., a 800x600 window becomes two 400x600 zones).The Logic: They must draw a thick black vertical line down the middle. Player 1's car, road, and enemies are confined to X coordinates between 0 and 400. Player 2's elements are confined to X coordinates between 400 and 800.The Pygame Trick: Advanced students can look into using standard Pygame Subsurfaces or clip rectangles (surface.set_clip()) to ensure Player 1's grass or enemies don't accidentally bleed over into Player 2's half of the screen.Option B: The "Shared Screen" Alternative (Much Easier but Fun)If true split-screen proves too frustrating due to Pygame Zero's rendering limits, have them build a shared-screen arcade racer (like Micro Machines or Super Mario Kart attributes but vertical).How it works: Both players are on the same wide road. They compete against each other and the AI simultaneously.The Rules: If Player 1 drives too fast and forces Player 2 off the bottom of the screen, Player 2 loses points or respawns with a time penalty.Why it's great: It requires zero advanced rendering math. They just add a second Actor (player2 = Actor('car_blue')), map it to keys.w, keys.a, keys.d, and calculate background scrolling based on the average speed of both players, or lock the camera to whoever is in first place.⏱️ Impact on the 6-Hour TimelineIf advanced students want to tackle Option A (Split-Screen), they will likely need an extra 1 to 2 hours of self-directed coding outside of the standard sessions to debug the clipping boundaries and coordinate offsets.If they tackle Option B (Shared Screen), they can easily implement it during the Session 6 "Polish & Customization" hour, as it only requires adding a few lines of code for Player 2's inputs and collisions.Would you like me to show you the code structural differences between handling a single-player track versus tracking dual-player data for either option?
